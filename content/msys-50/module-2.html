PART 2.1EA and Other Governance Instruments

The one idea to hold onto: Each framework manages one part of the organization. EA is the map of the whole organization, so every framework gets something from it. For each one, learn three things: what question it answers, what it is, and where EA helps.

General management

Strategic management

"How do we know our strategy is working, beyond just money?"

Balanced Scorecard (BSC)

Kaplan & Norton (1992) said financial numbers alone can't tell you if a strategy is working. So the BSC checks four perspectives, and each one has a mission, objectives, and measures.

CustomerFinancialInternal Business ProcessesLearning & Growth

Example (slide 7): A university measures graduation rate (Customer), cost per degree (Financial), teaching effectiveness (Processes), and faculty development (Learning & Growth).

FROM CLASS

Strategic planning produces the mission/vision statement, goals, and a SWOT analysis. The BSC then checks the strategy from four perspectives.

Where EA helps: the two highlighted perspectives. They are about how the organization works and grows (its processes, people, and technology), which is exactly what EA describes.

Strategy execution

"What is our business, and what must we be able to do?"

Business Model Canvas

A one-page template (Osterwalder & Pigneur, 2010) that sketches a business: its customers, what it offers, how it reaches them, and how it earns. It's easy to read, so it's used in brainstorming.

Business Architecture

A blueprint of the business side, so everyone shares one picture of the organization. It helps turn strategy into action, and the canvas can be its major input.

It uses capability-based planning: decide what abilities you need first, then how to deliver them.

Example: A university first decides it needs the ability to "deliver courses online" (what), and only then picks an LMS (how).

FROM CLASS

Capability examples from class: "buy and sell products online", "Ateneo can handle classes online". EA looks at current and future capabilities and what to enhance on both the business and IT side.

Where EA helps: many canvas elements can be turned into EA models, and business architecture is one of the domains inside EA.

Quality management

"Are we doing things well?"

EFQM

Broad. Compares the whole organization against best practices.

Its principles, measures, and indicators become the basis for continuous learning, innovation, and improvement. It complements the BSC.

Class example: You target 70% student retention, but best practice is 85%. EFQM raises your target.

Where EA helps: Policy & Strategy, and Processes.

From class: these two handle policy-making and how processes are implemented (actions and operations), which directly affect the EA.

ISO 9001

Narrow. Sets criteria for a good quality management system (QMS).

Key processes are the ones that affect product or service quality. Once the QMS is in place, you can request an audit to get certified.

Think of it as: a quality certificate, similar in spirit to school accreditation.

Where EA helps: identifying and documenting processes.

IT management

IT governance

"Is IT properly controlled and managed?"

COBIT

Control Objectives for Information and Related Technology. An IT control framework that connects business risks, control needs, and technical issues. It also gives success factors, goal indicators, and a maturity model for IT governance.

FROM CLASS

Concepts discussed: administration, predictability, information flow (guidance), restrictions, maintenance, rules and regulations, standards, and above all security. COBIT complements EA directly because it works at the actions/operations level.

Where EA helps: EA describes business and IT structures and processes. COBIT decides how IT should be organized. They fill each other's gaps.

IT delivery & support

"Are IT services delivered and supported well?"

ITIL

IT Infrastructure Library. A set of best-practice documents for good IT services, in two groups: service delivery and service support.

Class example: ISIS was rated about 6/10 for service delivery. ITIL is a tool to close that gap.

Where EA helps: EA gives a clear picture of IT, infrastructure, and processes. ITIL needs that to manage IT assets.

IT implementation

"How mature is our software development?"

CMMI

Capability Maturity Model Integration. Rates software engineering processes on 5 maturity levels and gives practices to move up. It replaced SW-CMM in 2000.

12345

Class example: When outsourcing, check the vendor's CMMI level. Level 5 means the highest quality to expect.

LevelNameMeaning
1Ad hoc (Initial)No structure in place
2Managed*Basic project management
3DefinedProcesses defined; EA useful from here
4Quantitatively managed*Processes measured and controlled
5OptimizedEverything in place and optimized

*Your notes left levels 2 and 4 blank; these are the standard CMMI names. CMMI is mainly an assessment tool.

Where EA helps: EA gives each software project its context, constraints, and guidelines. It is most useful from Level 3 up, once processes are defined.

COBIT vs. CMMICOBIT governs IT as a whole. CMMI rates software development maturity.
EFQM vs. ISO 9001EFQM is broad excellence vs. best practice. ISO 9001 is a narrow, certifiable quality system.
BSC vs. EFQMBSC sets your own targets. EFQM compares them to best practice.
Canvas vs. Business ArchitectureCanvas is a one-page sketch. Business architecture is the detailed blueprint.

Guide questions

Which BSC perspectives is EA particularly useful in?

Internal Business Processes and Learning & Growth (the last two of Customer, Financial, Internal Business Processes, Learning & Growth).

What is the Business Model Canvas? How is it useful?

A template by Osterwalder & Pigneur (2010) for a high-level description of new or existing business models. It has an intuitive layout, works well in brainstorming, its elements can be translated into EA models, and it can be a major input to business architecture.

Your slide says "7 parts," but the standard canvas and the slide's own example show 9 blocks: key partners, key activities, key resources, value propositions, customer relationships, channels, customer segments, cost structure, revenue streams. Follow your prof's wording if asked.
Describe the nature of business architecture.
  • A blueprint that gives a common understanding of the organization, used to align strategic objectives and tactical demands.
  • A business-oriented abstraction that helps translate strategy into action.
  • Uses capability-based planning: focus on what (current and desired capabilities) before how.
  • The Business Model Canvas can be a major input. It is an important domain in the broader EA scope.
How does EFQM encourage continuous learning and innovation?

It benchmarks the organization against best practices and gives principles, measures, and indicators for assessing performance. These assessments become the basis for continuous learning, innovation, and improvement. The "Innovation & Learning" arrow feeds Results back into the Enablers. Class example: the org targets 70% retention, but best practice is 85%.

What are key processes under ISO 9001?

Processes that affect product or service quality, including product realization (the business processes that realize the company's product or service).

How does EA complement COBIT?

EA focuses on business and IT structures and processes. COBIT focuses on how IT should be organized and controlled. Together they cover both the structure and its governance.

Explain the relationship between ITIL, COBIT, and EA.

COBIT's control objectives tell what to do. ITIL explains how. EA provides a clear understanding of IT, infrastructure, and business processes, which ITIL needs to manage IT assets.

How is CMMI different from COBIT?

CMMI assesses the maturity of software engineering processes (IT implementation) across 5 levels, and applies to in-house teams or outsourced vendors. COBIT is an IT governance and control framework. It also has a maturity model, but for IT governance, not software development.

PART 2.2The Problem of Business and IT Alignment

Strategic management produces a high-level business strategy: mission, vision & values, strategic goals & objectives, competitive and environmental analysis (e.g., SWOT), and KPIs.

Why business strategy is inadequate for IT planning

#ProblemMeaning
1Generic, vague"Be the leading ISP in the country." Open to interpretation, may not be documented.
2No IT suggestionsNo practical IT direction; IT planners decide by guesswork. Little value as input to IT and EA.
3UnstableFrequent changes give no solid basis; priorities shift and wreck IT plans.
4Short-term systemsSystems only help in the short term; assets become liabilities under the next strategy; legacy systems pile up → alignment trap.
FROM CLASS

Alignment trap, in plain terms: hiring people and creating departments only for one goal or system. When that strategy gets scrapped, they're no longer used.

Operating model quadrants

Two levels: process standardization (x) and data integration (y). From Ross et al. (2006).

Data integration →
CoordinationLow std · High integ
Decentralized; diverse, interdependent units share data. Cross-/up-selling. BDO, Disney
UnificationHigh std · High integ
Centralized; similar, interdependent units. Consistent experience. Airlines
DiversificationLow std · Low integ

FROM CLASS
  • Diversification: acquiring businesses unrelated to the core business.
  • Replication: the same business processes from one unit to another, so every unit is consistent (hotel chains, restaurants).
  • Coordination (Disney): low standardization because of many different companies (hotels, theme parks, film studio); high integration because of shared branding and customer experience.
  • Unification (airlines): experience and data must be consistent; one centralized unit.
  • Can a business switch quadrants? Yes. Amazon started in Unification (selling books) and moved to Coordination.

Your notes describe Coordination as "independent business units", but slide 15 says diverse, interdependent units. Go with the slide.

Guide questions

Mission vs. vision and values

Mission: the organization's purpose now (what it does and for whom). Vision: where it wants to be in the future. Values: guiding principles for how it behaves.

Standard definitions; the slides only list these as outputs.

What is SWOT?

Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats: a competitive and environmental analysis produced by strategic management.

Two reasons strategic management output is inadequate for IT planning

Any two of the four problems above: vague, no IT suggestions, unstable, non-reusable short-term systems (alignment trap).

What is the alignment trap?

Focusing on new systems for the current strategy. When the strategy changes, those systems become isolated legacy systems that pile up and turn from assets into liabilities, hurting IT agility.

What is the middle ground between business and IT?

Key discussion points that are meaningful and valuable to both sides. Business can see the value of IT decisions; IT gets actionable suggestions. It should be valuable in the short (<1–2 yrs), long (3–5 yrs), and very long term (>3–5 yrs). Purely business topics (market segmentation) mean nothing to IT; purely IT topics (relational databases) mean nothing to business.

Two levels that are the basis of an operating model

Process standardization and data integration.

What does process standardization mean?

How far business units perform the same processes in the same way across the organization. High standardization gives a consistent customer experience and efficient processes.

How is Diversification the opposite of Unification?

Diversification is low standardization, low integration, decentralized, with diverse independent units. Unification is high on both, centralized, with similar interdependent units that share data.

Why is the operating model a good discussion point?

It is the most abstract and stable point, with a very long planning horizon, so IT built around it is not wasted when strategy shifts. It shapes reporting structure and autonomy (business side) and implies which EA domains to standardize and which data to integrate (IT side).

How does a business capability encompass processes and other resources?

A capability is the general ability to perform an activity. It includes the underlying business processes, people, knowledge, and other resources that make it possible, and forms a stable building block of business functions.

Explain the hierarchy of the five discussion points.

See the pyramid table. Operating model is the base (widest scope, longest horizon, organization-wide). Business requirements are the top (narrowest, 6–12 months, initiative-specific).

PART 2.3The IT Initiative

The one idea to hold onto: An IT initiative is just an IT project (or a bundle of projects) that delivers a new system or changes existing IT so the business gets better. Part 2.3 asks three things: what makes up an initiative, how EA helps pick and deliver them, and which of the five types an initiative is.

1 · What an IT initiative is

The key unit of work in EA practice. It ends in either a deployed IT solution (systems development) or a change in the IT landscape (infrastructure). Its usual purpose is to solve a specific business need. It can be one project, or several projects that share one purpose and one business case.

Link to 2.2: in the pyramid, specific business needs are where IT initiatives come from. Operating model → business capabilities → specific business needs (initiatives) → business processes → business requirements.

2 · Its six attributes

Think of these as the checklist for pitching the project to management.

Business needWhat problem are we solving?
Goals & objectivesWhat will be better when it's done?
ScopeWhat's included, and what isn't? Which stakeholders will use the system?
Business caseIs it worth it? Initial investment, ROI.
Business sponsorsWhich executives back it?
BudgetHow much money is approved?

3 · How EA helps: the what and the how

The what

Which initiatives should we do?

List the current, in-pipeline, and future initiatives, then prioritize them into a timeline: which to propose first and which to deploy first. Goal: the optimal portfolio for maximum business value.

The how

How should we deliver each one?

Pick the optimal way to deliver: build it in-house, outsource it, or buy off-the-shelf / use open source (and customize if needed).

4 · The five types

First, sort by who proposes it.

Top-down from executives and architects
FundamentalStrategicArchitectural
Bottom-up from departments and operations
LocalUrgent

Memory trick: bottom-up types come from people on the ground: a department with a Local need, or a sudden Urgent problem. "LU" = bottom-up. Everything else is top-down.

Fundamental · top-down

"We'll always need this, whatever our strategy is."

Fundamental

Covers permanent business needs. It's strategy-agnostic: always necessary and useful, no matter what strategy the company follows. Identified by C-level executives and architects, often from the operating model's requirements.

Class examples: ISIS (student information system), a bank's account management system, an insurer's agent management system.

From class: fundamental initiatives stay the same whatever the operating model; they change only if the organization changes industry.

Spot it: if the core business would stop working without it, it's fundamental.

Strategic · top-down

"We need this to execute our current strategy."

Strategic

Comes straight from the current business strategy, often proposed by top executives for immediate impact on executing it.

Caveat: if the strategy changes, the system can become a legacy system. That's the alignment trap from 2.2.

Example: A university builds a system to support a new strategy of recruiting international students. If that strategy is dropped, the system loses its purpose.

From class: because strategies can shift, strategic initiatives have no solid foundation. They are judged with risk assessment (probabilities): high risk, high reward. If it works, it gives the company a competitive advantage.

Spot it: ask "would this still matter if the strategy changed?" If no, it's strategic.

Local · bottom-up

"Our department needs this for our own work."

Local

Addresses a specific local need and may be unrelated to the organization's strategy. Proposed by local executives. Top executives and architects review it by how important it is to the whole organization, and approved ones get prioritized.

Class example: One department pilots an automated manuscript formatter, then proposes it college-wide.

Spot it: it starts in one unit, for that unit's problem.

Urgent · bottom-up

"Something happened and we need this now."

Urgent

Comes from urgent needs: overlooked critical problems, new government regulations, new operational demands. It's unanticipated, unpredictable, unplanned, and usually triggered by the external environment. In reality there are a lot of them.

Class examples: forced LMS adoption during the pandemic; RFID sensors on campus.

Spot it: nobody planned it. An outside event forced it.

Architectural · top-down

"Our IT itself needs fixing or upgrading."

Architectural

Improves the overall quality of the IT landscape. Unlike the other four, it does not directly address a business need or add new business functionality. Proposed by architects.

Class examples: upgrading obsolete hardware, decommissioning legacy systems, replacing faulty Wi-Fi hotspots.

Spot it: users get no new feature. Only the IT behind the scenes improves.

Which type is it? Ask in this order

  1. Does it add business value directly, or only improve the IT itself?Only IT → Architectural
  2. Was it unplanned and forced by an outside event (crisis, new regulation)?Yes → Urgent
  3. Did one department propose it for its own needs?Yes → Local
  4. Is it tied to the current strategy, so it would lose purpose if the strategy changed?Yes → Strategic
  5. Is it needed no matter what the strategy is?Yes → Fundamental

An initiative can fit more than one type. Your prof accepts that as long as you justify it (e.g., a department's tool that's also urgent because of a new regulation).

Guide questions

What is an IT initiative? What are its characteristics?

The key unit of work in EA practice, resulting in a deployed IT solution or a change in the IT landscape that enables business improvements. Usually addresses a specific business need; can be single or multi-project. Attributes: business need, goals and objectives, scope, business case (investment, ROI), business sponsors, budget.

How does EA help in dealing with IT initiatives?

It helps decide what initiatives to implement (the optimal portfolio for maximum business value, prioritized into a timeline) and how to implement them (the optimal delivery: in-house, outsourcing, or off-the-shelf/open source).

What are the five types?

Fundamental, Strategic, Local, Urgent, Architectural.

Why consider strategic initiatives despite the caveats?

They directly support executing the current strategy, with immediate impact, and can bring competitive advantage. The risk of becoming legacy is worth taking when success is likely (class example: around 90%) and the potential advantage is significant.

Which two types are proposed bottom-up?

Local and Urgent.
