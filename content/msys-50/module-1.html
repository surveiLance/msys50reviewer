PART 1.1Overview of EA

The one idea to hold onto: EA is a holistic, integrated view of business and IT. It's a practice (with principles, documents, and models) that describes where the organization is now and where it wants to be, so business and IT can plan together.

Three definitions

Lankhorst

"A coherent whole of principles, methods, and models that are used in the design and realisation of an enterprise's organisational structure, business processes, information systems, and infrastructure."

Key words: principles, methods, models · design and realisation

Kotusev

"A collection of special documents (artifacts) describing various aspects of an organization from an integrated business and IT perspective intended to bridge the communication gap between business and IT stakeholders, facilitate information systems planning and thereby improve business and IT alignment."

Key words: artifacts · communication gap · IS planning · alignment

Bernard

"The analysis and documentation of an enterprise in its current and future states from an integrated strategy, business, and technology perspective."

Key words: current and future states · strategy, business, technology

Common standpoint (slide 8): holistic · integrated · business · IS/IT · perspective · principles · practice · documents.

FROM CLASS

Reading the definitions: Lankhorst is about capturing requirements from both the business and IT side. Bernard goes further and captures requirements for the future state too ("what does the organization want to be?"), across short-, medium-, and long-term timelines.

Common standpoint, explained: perspective: there are many different ones · holistic: deals with the entire organization · documents: the many outputs EA produces, called artifacts.

The architecture process is a lifecycle

Architecture is a process as well as a product. It goes from idea → operational system → modify/replace, so it's more of a lifecycle (Lankhorst).

Management loops back to a new Idea, so the cycle never ends.

FROM CLASS

The architecture is produced in the form of artifacts and models. The process is not linear: you can start anywhere in the cycle, and it repeats (iterative).

Guide questions

How do you understand EA based on the different definitions?

All three share a common standpoint: EA is a holistic, integrated view of the business and IS/IT. Lankhorst stresses principles, methods, and models for designing the enterprise. Kotusev stresses documents (artifacts) that bridge the communication gap between business and IT and improve alignment. Bernard stresses documenting the current and future states from a strategy, business, and technology perspective.

Why is city planning an accurate analog of EA?

Both are complex, multifaceted practices. Cities and organizations can't be perfectly planned in every detail, keep running while they evolve, change only through incremental modifications, and never stop evolving. The roles also match: business executives ↔ city governors, IT project teams ↔ construction teams, architects ↔ city planners.

Why is effective communication a critical aspect of EA?

EA requires involvement of the whole enterprise and mutual understanding of diverse perspectives. Each domain is developed by different stakeholders with their own concerns and language. Kotusev's definition says EA exists to bridge the communication gap between business and IT, and architects act as intermediaries between stakeholders.

What are EA architects?

The chief IT planners and owners of EA artifacts. They know both business and IT but have an IT-centric background, and they are effective communicators, team players, and innovators (1.3).

What is a stakeholder?

A person or group with an interest (concern) in the enterprise and its architecture, such as the major actors listed above. In 3.2, the stakeholder is the "viewer" whose interests and background shape how they see the enterprise. Stakeholders can be individuals or groups (e.g., one business manager, or all of business management).

The 1.1 slides list the actors but don't define "stakeholder"; this combines the general meaning with 3.2.

Why is it more appropriate to call the architecture process a life cycle?

Because architecture is a process as well as a product. It moves from an idea, to design, to use, to management, and then changes lead to new ideas that modify or replace it. It is never finished, which matches EA's characteristic that it continuously evolves.

PART 1.2EA Drivers

The one idea to hold onto: Organizations feel pressure from inside (internal drivers, mainly the need for alignment) and from outside (external drivers like regulations). Good architectural practice becomes indispensable as the organization grows and becomes complex.
Internal drivers

Pressures within the organization.

Main one: organizational alignment, the well-orchestrated interaction of organizational components (hard and soft). Business-IT alignment is especially crucial.

External drivers

Pressures outside the organization.

May trigger a push for adoption of an EA practice. External regulations generate substantial impact.

Hard vs. soft organizational components

HardWell documented and formally disseminated; gives structure (e.g., job descriptions).
SoftWord of mouth and the work environment; hard to measure (culture, friendships between employees, attitude).
FROM CLASS

Good architectural practice means creating a roadmap that helps the organization with its future growth. External driver example: disaster preparedness, like schools during the pandemic.

Sample scenario · tap to revealThe central bank requires all banks to report transactions digitally. A bank starts an EA practice to cope. What kind of driver is this?
External driverIt comes from outside the organization, and external regulations generate substantial impact and can push an organization to adopt EA (1.2 slide 11).

Strategic Alignment Model (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993)

Distinguishes business strategy and organizational infrastructure from IT strategy and IT infrastructure. Gives four dominant perspectives used for alignment.

Strategic fitThe vertical link: external (strategy) ↔ internal (infrastructure and processes).
Functional integrationThe horizontal link: business ↔ IT.
FROM CLASS

Functional integration mixes the business side and the IT side. Strategic fit means the internal side must be able to support the strategy on every side.

Sample scenario · tap to revealThe business strategy is about growing online sales, but the IT strategy is only about cutting hardware costs. Which SAM link is weak?
Functional integrationThe mismatch is between the business side and the IT side at the strategy level: the horizontal link.
Sample scenario · tap to revealIT plans to move to cloud services, but the current IT infrastructure can't support it. Which SAM link is broken?
Strategic fitThe mismatch is between a strategy (external) and its infrastructure (internal): the vertical link.

Nadler et al. (1992)

Labovitz & Rosansky (1997)

Organizational alignment

Vertical: top strategy ↔ people at the bottom (are they in touch with each other?).
Horizontal: internal processes ↔ external customers.

Don't mix up: in the SAM, the vertical link is called strategic fit. In Labovitz & Rosansky, vertical alignment is strategy ↔ people. Different models, different meanings.

EA is positioned within the context of managing the enterprise. Architecture is not a static asset.

EA role (Ross et al., 2006)

Successful organizations employ an operating model (Coordination, Unification, Diversification, Replication; see Module 2). EA serves as the organizing logic for business processes and IT infrastructure, and must reflect the integration and standardization requirements.

Guide questions

What does organizational alignment mean?

The well-orchestrated interaction of organizational components, both hard and soft (e.g., Nadler's work, people, formal and informal organization). It can be vertical (strategy to people) or horizontal (processes to customers). Business-IT alignment is especially crucial.

Differentiate internal and external drivers.

Internal drivers are pressures within the organization, mainly the need for organizational and business-IT alignment. External drivers are pressures outside the organization, such as regulations, which generate substantial impact and may push the organization to adopt an EA practice.

What is the Strategic Alignment Model of Henderson and Venkatraman?

A 1993 model that distinguishes business strategy, organizational infrastructure, IT strategy, and IT infrastructure. It aligns them through strategic fit (external strategy ↔ internal infrastructure) and functional integration (business ↔ IT), giving four dominant perspectives for alignment.

Why is architecture not a static asset?

EA sits inside the management of the enterprise: it is one of the actions that move the organization from its as-is to its to-be goals. Since strategy, goals, and operations keep changing, the architecture has to keep changing with them. EA continuously evolves.

FROM CLASS

Operating model: the structure for how an organization operates; an organization probably belongs in one of the quadrants. Process standardization = how similar the processes are. Data integration = how accessible data is across business units and how well it's used in each department's outputs.

PART 1.3The Complexity of EA

The one idea to hold onto: An organization is a living system, not a building. You can't design it once and build it. Like a city, it keeps running while it changes, so EA works like city planning.

Understanding organizational complexity

  • EA is unlike building architecture.
  • Organizations are dynamic socio-technical systems: they cannot be engineered then built. They are complex, organic, living entities that gradually evolve over time.
  • Successful organizations are well-managed evolutions (well-organized).

EA and city planning

Business executives↔ City governors
IT project teams↔ Construction teams
Architects↔ City planners

What organizations and cities have in common (slides 9–10):

  • Cannot be perfectly planned in every detail
  • Future needs can be anticipated but not described in detail
  • Running and evolving at the same time
  • Cannot be changed entirely, only through incremental modifications
  • Major changes do not happen overnight
  • Poor planning decisions can significantly hinder further development
  • Evolution is endless
  • No single best way to evolve
  • Planning must consider multiple concerns

Common language, models, and frameworks

Common language

Use of a common language is crucial. No need to start from scratch: it's better to use existing elements when possible.

Models as representation

Models provide abstract, unambiguous definitions; they are formal and come in many types. They help capture the essence of EA. Only the core of each domain should be included, and multiple models are usually needed.

Architectural frameworks

Integrate different elements; many are available for EA. Ask: Is it applicable and/or useful to the organization? Is it worth adopting one? If not, what is the alternative?

From class: a framework is a template or guide. There are also non-framework approaches (Kotusev). See Module 4.1.

Guide questions

Why must EA architects be effective communicators and team players?

They are intermediaries between diverse stakeholders, each with their own concerns and language. EA needs the whole enterprise involved and mutual understanding of diverse perspectives, so architects must translate between groups and work with them, not alone.

Explain how organizations and cities go through never-ending evolution.

Both keep running and evolving at the same time. They can't be changed entirely, only through incremental modifications, and major changes don't happen overnight. Future needs can be anticipated but not described in detail, there's no single best way to evolve, and the evolution is endless.

Why is the bottom-up approach an effective alignment strategy?

Each domain is developed by distinct stakeholders with their own concerns and language. Starting from those domains and stakeholders builds shared understanding from the ground up, instead of forcing one view from the top that the domain owners don't recognize. The slide states alignment "needs bottom-up process."

In your opinion, which domains may be present in many EA practices?

Infrastructure appears in both Kotusev and Bernard. Business/process, data/information, and applications/systems also show up in all three lists under different names (Business ≈ Workflows ≈ Process; Data ≈ Dataflows ≈ Information; Applications ≈ Systems ≈ Application; Infrastructure ≈ Technical).

Why include just the core elements of each domain in EA models?

EA covers the whole enterprise, so full detail would make models unmanageable and hard for diverse stakeholders to understand. The essence of each domain is of more value to EA, and the focus should be on the relations between domains (3.2).
