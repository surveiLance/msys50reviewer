PART 4.1Architecture Approaches, Methods, and Frameworks

The one idea to hold onto: A framework helps organize architectural work, but using one does not guarantee success. Distinguish Zachman's classification, MDA's model mappings, and SOA's services. Use what serves the enterprise's actual needs.

Lesson source: Part 4.1 slides 4–19. Main explanations follow the supplied slides; class-note clarifications and original practice examples are labeled separately. Diagrams are original redrawings, not slide screenshots.

Frameworks and architecture approaches

A framework is an organizing structure for architectural work: it identifies and relates viewpoints and modeling techniques, and may also supply processes, deliverables, or metrics. A framework is not necessarily a step-by-step method.

Framework-basedUse an established structure and, where provided, its method. Tailor it to the problem.
Non-framework-basedOrganize the work using other practical approaches rather than adopting a complete framework.
HybridCombine useful elements—for example, Zachman's artifact classification with TOGAF's ADM.

Whatever the approach, keep relations between architectural domains clear and track and carry out changes methodically. On slide 6, Lankhorst says adoption of a particular framework is insufficient; Kotusev offers the stronger criticism that popular EA frameworks are products of marketing specialists and management gurus.

In plain language: adopting a framework is not evidence that architectural work has succeeded. Consider its usefulness in context rather than treating compliance as the outcome.

Zachman: classify artifacts by perspective and question

Introduced by John Zachman in 1987 as the Framework for Information Systems Architecture, it is a logical structure for classifying and organizing descriptions of an enterprise. A cell is the intersection of a role/perspective and a product abstraction/question.

What → DataEntities and information: students, courses, records.
How → FunctionProcesses: admission, enrollment, grading.
Where → NetworkLocations and distribution: campuses, sites, networks.
Who → PeopleActors, roles, organizational responsibilities.
When → TimeSchedules, events, cycles, deadlines.
Why → MotivationGoals, policies, strategies, business rules.

  • Addresses the enterprise as a whole.

Disadvantages — slide 12

  • Relationships between cells are not well specified.

Explanation: classification alone is not a step-by-step implementation method.

Use pragmatically: choose focus areas and priorities, work top-down or bottom-up, collect useful existing artifacts, identify gaps, and begin with a minimum viable architecture—the cells critical for decisions. You do not need to fill every cell before doing useful work.

OMG's Model-Driven Architecture: CIM, PIM, PSM

Model-Driven Architecture (MDA), from the Object Management Group (OMG), aims for an open, vendor-neutral approach to interoperability. It raises the abstraction level at which software solutions are specified. The lecture emphasizes that it is too IT-centric to cover enterprise architecture on its own: its focus is software development and deployment.

CIMBusiness/domain model and business requirements. Describe the problem without committing to a computational implementation.
PIMDescribe solution logic independently of a particular platform—for example, a workflow independent of its workflow engine.
PSMSpecify the solution for a chosen platform—for example, a Java-platform model.

Mapping is the key mechanism: rules and techniques translate one model into another. Do not confuse MDA's abstraction levels with TOGAF's architectural domains.

Service-Oriented Architecture and its EA significance

SOA is a set of design principles that lets units of functionality be provided and consumed as services. It applies to business as well as software: a business service offers a value proposition within a value chain or business process.

SOA separates external behavior (what a service provides to its consumer) from internal behavior (how the provider implements it). The consumer usually needs the service outcome and interface, not the provider's internal workings.

Class-note clarification: a provider may also consume another provider's service. Original example: a payment provider can consume an identity-verification service while serving a retailer.

Why EA cares: “Service” can be understood across domains. Services promote interoperability, flexibility, and cost effectiveness; they can be reused or combined and encourage new ways of organizing work.
Original practice scenarioAdmissions and the library both consume a student-verification service without knowing its internal database logic. What concept explains this?
SOA: service reuse and separation of external from internal behavior.The departments rely on the provided functionality, while the provider controls its implementation.

Guide questions — Part 4.1

What is a framework?

An organizing structure that relates architectural viewpoints and associated modeling techniques; it may additionally provide processes, deliverables, and metrics. It structures work but does not guarantee effective architecture.

Describe the essence of the Zachman Framework. What are its advantages and disadvantages?

It classifies enterprise descriptions at intersections of perspectives (such as Owner, Designer, Builder) and questions (What, How, Where, Who, When, Why). It is understandable, enterprise-wide, tool/method independent, and helps locate concerns. Its many cells and weakly specified cross-cell relations are limitations. Start with decision-critical cells, not automatic completion of the whole grid.

Describe OMG's Model-Driven Architecture.

MDA is OMG's vendor-neutral, interoperability-oriented approach to specifying software through models at three abstraction levels: CIM, PIM, and PSM. Mappings translate between levels. It is valuable for software development/deployment but too IT-centric to represent all of EA alone.

What is Service-Oriented Architecture? What is its significance to EA?

SOA provides and consumes functionality as services, separating externally offered behavior from internal implementation. Because services apply to both business and software domains, they help connect those domains, enable reuse/composition, and improve interoperability, flexibility, and cost effectiveness.

PART 4.2The Open Group Architecture Framework (TOGAF)

The one idea to hold onto: TOGAF gives an adaptable approach to EA. Know its four components, four domains, and especially the iterative ADM. The lecture's ADM diagrams use the TOGAF 9.1 depiction; learn the version presented for this quiz.

Lesson source: Part 4.2 slides 4–12, with TOGAF background from 4.1 slide 14. The diagrams below retain the supplied TOGAF 9.1 depiction; class-note clarifications and original examples are identified separately.

TOGAF: adaptable, not all-or-nothing

The Open Group Architecture Framework originated as a generic framework and methodology for technical architectures. It treats the enterprise as a system and, from version 8 onward, is dedicated to EA. The standard is extensive and modular, can be combined with other standards or approaches, supports incremental adoption, and offers certification.

Class emphasis: begin with what the enterprise actually needs. Combining Zachman's classification with ADM is possible; adopting every component immediately is not required.

The four main components

Architecture Capability FrameworkOrganization, processes, skills, roles, and responsibilities needed to establish and operate the EA practice. Think: “Can we perform EA?”
Architecture Development Method (ADM)The iterative development method. Think: “How do we develop, govern, and evolve architecture?”
Architecture Content FrameworkOrganizes architectural content; the lesson emphasizes closely interrelated Business, Data, Application, and Technology architectures. Think: “What are we describing?”
Enterprise ContinuumPlaces architectures in broader context: foundational → common systems → industry-specific → enterprise-specific. Think: “How generic or specific is this architectural asset?”

In plain language: the “Think” questions above are memory aids, not additional component names. Slides 5, 7–8 give the four components and their descriptions.

Do not confuse the Architecture Capability Framework (the organization's ability to do EA) with a Business Capability Model (a CSVLOD Visions subtype describing what the business can do).

The four architectural domains

BusinessStrategy, governance, organization, and key business processes.
DataLogical/physical data assets and data management.
ApplicationIndividual applications, their deployment and interactions, and relationships to core business processes.
TechnologyIT infrastructure and hardware/software capabilities supporting deployment.

Original example: the enrollment approval process is Business; student-record structure and stewardship are Data; interactions between the enrollment and billing applications are Application; server/network capabilities are Technology. Class-note clarification: security can cut across these domains rather than replacing one of the four.

ADM characteristics and the phase map

The Architecture Development Method is iterative, not an inflexible one-pass sequence. At every iteration, make fresh decisions on enterprise coverage, level of detail, time horizon, and architectural assets to leverage. Customize it where needed; it can be used alongside frameworks such as Zachman.

PhaseFocus in the supplied slides
PreliminaryPrepare architecture capability and customize the framework.
A — Architecture VisionEstablish vision, scope/limitations, stakeholders, and approvals.
B — Business ArchitectureDevelop Business architecture based on the vision.
C — Information Systems ArchitecturesDevelop Data and Application architectures.
D — Technology ArchitectureDevelop Technology architecture.
E — Opportunities and SolutionsInitial implementation planning and identification of delivery vehicles.
F — Migration PlanningPlan how to move from baseline to target architecture.
G — Implementation GovernanceProvide architectural oversight of implementation.
H — Architecture Change ManagementManage changes to the architecture.
Requirements Management — throughoutThe central, ongoing activity connected to the phases—not a final phase after H.
Quiz trap: C = Data + Application; D = Technology. E identifies solution/delivery opportunities; F plans migration; G governs implementation; H manages architecture change. Requirements management stays central across the cycle.
Original practice scenarioA team already has a target architecture. It now plans transition from the current environment, then checks whether implementation follows the architecture. Which phases?
F — Migration Planning, then G — Implementation Governance.Planning the transition is different from overseeing implementation conformance.

Guide questions — Part 4.2

What are the main components of TOGAF?

Architecture Capability Framework; Architecture Development Method (ADM); Architecture Content Framework; Enterprise Continuum. Respectively: establish the EA practice, perform the iterative method, organize architectural content, and understand generic-to-enterprise-specific context.

What are the four architectural domains supported by TOGAF?

Business (strategy, governance, organization, processes); Data (assets and management); Application (applications, interactions, process support); Technology (infrastructure and hardware/software capabilities). Their relationships matter: they are not isolated descriptions.

Describe the characteristics of the TOGAF ADM.

ADM is iterative, tailorable, and compatible with other frameworks. Each iteration revisits breadth of coverage, detail, time horizon, and reusable architectural assets. Preliminary work establishes capability; A–D establish vision and architectures; E–F plan solutions and transition; G governs implementation; H manages change. Requirements Management connects throughout.

PART 4.3The CSVLOD Model

The one idea to hold onto: First ask Rules, Structures, or Changes? Then ask business-focused or IT-focused? Those two decisions identify the six CSVLOD types. Subtypes are tools to choose pragmatically, not a checklist of 24 compulsory documents.

Lesson source: Part 4.3 slides 4–24. Popularity labels and horizons follow the supplied lesson, not claims about every organization. Supplementary references and original practice examples are labeled separately.

Two dimensions produce six artifact types

CSVLOD means Considerations, Standards, Visions, Landscapes, Outlines, Designs. It is a taxonomy—an evidence-based classification of EA artifacts.

Rules — slide 6Broad, global prescriptions defining an organization or division. Often textual; permanent but periodically updated. Support consistent planning decisions. Answer: “How do we work or want to work?” Lecture example: requiring Linux on all servers.
Structures — slide 7High-level abstract instances and their relationships. Usually graphical; relatively stable and continuously updated. Answer: “What approximately do we have or want to have?” Lecture example: describing IT systems in the context of business capabilities.
Changes — slide 8Proposed incremental changes, with concrete instances described in detail. Usually mixed text/graphics; temporary for a specific purpose, then discarded. Lecture example: business-process changes required when implementing a new IT system.

Business-focused — Dimension 2

Usually technology-neutral, plain business language, “soft” informal descriptions, only essential information for an executive-level audience.

IT-focused — Dimension 2

Technical, IT-specific, “hard” formal descriptions for architects and IT specialists. Internal IT tools generally not used by business audiences.

Dual artifacts bridge business and IT understanding: Considerations, Visions, Outlines. Business-focused does not mean irrelevant to IT. Standards, Landscapes, Designs are IT-focused and not dual in this model.

Considerations — business-focused rules

Global rules important to business and relevant to IT, expressed in simple, intuitive formats. They establish the organizational context for IS planning. They are dual because both audiences use them. Proper use leads to conceptual consistency between business and IT.

Principles — essentialHigh-level guidelines for planning and decision-making.
Policies — commonOrganizational norms with compulsory prescriptions in particular areas.
Conceptual Data Models — uncommonMain business-critical entities and their relationships, at a business-understandable conceptual level.
Analytical Reports — label not statedExecutive-level analyses of technology trends and their impact.
Direction Statements — uncommonMessages communicating major organization-wide decisions.

A principle guides a decision; a policy prescribes compulsory behavior. A business-level Conceptual Data Model belongs here; a technical Logical Data Model belongs under Standards.

Standards — IT-focused rules

Global technical rules and best practices provide proven, reusable means for implementation. These are not dual artifacts: they use technical detail for an IT audience. They support technical consistency, technological homogeneity, and regulatory compliance. Proper use enables faster delivery of new IT initiatives.

Technology Reference Models — essentialGraphical representations of technologies used in the organization.
Guidelines — essentialImplementation-level prescriptions for technology-specific domains.
Patterns — commonReusable solutions to recurring IT-system design problems.
IT Principles — commonGlobal guidelines influencing IT-related decisions.
Logical Data Models — uncommonLogical- or physical-level entities and their relationships.

Visions — business-focused structures

High-level conceptual descriptions from the business perspective. They are dual and focus on the long term, 3–5 years ahead: what IT should deliver for long-term business outcomes. Formats are brief and informal, often one-page diagrams.

Business Capability Models — essentialBusiness capabilities with their relationships and hierarchy.
Roadmaps — essentialPlanned IT initiatives with direct business value.
Target States — commonDesired long-term future state of the organization.
Value Chains — uncommonHow activities contribute added value.
Context Diagrams — uncommonHigh-level descriptions of current operational flows.

Two essential subtypes: Business Capability Models and Roadmaps. Do not classify only by “future” or “current”: Context Diagrams describe current flows but are still classified under Visions in this lesson. Focus, audience, and purpose matter together.

Landscapes — IT-focused structures

High-level technical descriptions of the IT landscape, often of the current state, in strict formats such as one-page diagrams. They are not dual. Proper use increases reuse, reduces asset duplication, improves IT agility, and decreases dependence on legacy systems.

Landscape Diagrams — essentialTechnical boxes-and-arrows views of the IT environment.
Inventories — commonCatalogs of available IT assets and essential properties.
Enterprise Systems Portfolios — commonHigh-level mappings of important IT systems to business capabilities.
IT Roadmaps — commonPlanned IT initiatives from a purely technical perspective, such as platform upgrades.
Roadmap trap: Visions' Roadmaps communicate direct business value; Landscapes' IT Roadmaps communicate technical evolution. Landscapes often describe the present, but “often” does not exclude technical future plans.

Outlines — business-focused changes

High-level descriptions of specific IT initiatives understandable to business leaders. They are dual, focus on the mid-term future, 1–2 years ahead, and mix text with simple diagrams. They support estimates of business value and impact. Proper use improves efficiency and return on IT investments.

Solution Overviews — essentialHigh-level descriptions of proposed IT solutions understandable to business leaders.
Options Assessments — commonEvaluate alternatives for a specific IT initiative—for example, compare two feasible delivery options and their trade-offs.
Initiative Proposals — uncommonEarly, idea-level descriptions and justifications for proposed initiatives.

Designs — IT-focused changes

Detailed technical descriptions of separate, approved IT projects, actionable for project teams. They are not dual. They focus on the short term, up to one year ahead, using text, tables, and complex diagrams. Proper use improves the quality of IT project delivery.

Solution Designs — essential

Detailed technical and functional specifications of approved IT solutions.

Preliminary Solution Designs — uncommon

Higher-level technical and functional designs of approved IT solutions.

Main difference = level of detail. Both concern approved solutions. “Preliminary” here does not mean an unapproved, early business idea—that would be closer to an Initiative Proposal under Outlines.

Putting the six types together

Supplementary explanation: the six cells also support connected planning work, not just naming documents. Business leaders and architects use Considerations to guide decisions and Visions to guide investments. Standards constrain implementation choices; Landscapes show the existing environment and inform new initiatives. Outlines support evaluation, approval, and funding of specific initiatives; Designs support implementation. This is a set of interacting roles, not a mandatory one-pass sequence.

Original application example: a capability gap motivates an initiative; an overview supports the investment decision; the design respects standards and existing-system connections. After delivery, landscape information needs updating. Learn which artifact helps which person make which decision, not just the acronym.

Supplementary reference: Kotusev's BCS presentation, usage, lifecycle, and process views. Secondhand maturity-level labels and administrative announcements are deliberately not added as quiz facts.

Original practice scenarioA retailer has a business-wide customer-information policy, approved database technology rules, a four-year capability plan, an application inventory, a comparison of two proposed checkout solutions, and detailed build specifications. Classify each.

Guide questions — Part 4.3

What does the CSVLOD acronym mean?

Considerations, Standards, Visions, Landscapes, Outlines, Designs—the six general types of EA artifacts.

What are the two major dimensions of the CSVLOD approach?

Dimension 1, “What?”, distinguishes Rules, Structures, and Changes. Dimension 2, “How?”, distinguishes Business-focused and IT-focused descriptions, language, and audiences.

Describe the Rules, Structures, and Changes part of Dimension 1.

Rules prescribe how the organization works or wants to work, are often textual, and persist with periodic updates. Structures describe high-level elements and relationships, are usually graphical, and remain relatively stable with ongoing updates. Changes describe concrete proposed incremental changes, combine text/graphics, and are temporary for a specific purpose.

How did the two dimensions determine the taxonomy of CSVLOD?

Crossing three “What?” categories with two focus categories gives six cells. Business-focused rules/structures/changes are Considerations/Visions/Outlines; IT-focused rules/structures/changes are Standards/Landscapes/Designs.

How are the subtypes further classified?

By observed popularity: Essential (>50% of EA practices), Common (25–50%), Uncommon (10–25%). The lesson reports 24 subtypes, eight in each popularity group. These categories describe prevalence, not mandatory document counts.

Why are Considerations considered as dual artifacts?

They express global rules that are important for business and relevant to IT in simple, shared formats. Both sides use them as organizational context for planning, promoting conceptual consistency.

What are EA artifacts under Standards? Why are they not considered as dual artifacts?

Technology Reference Models, Guidelines, Patterns, IT Principles, and Logical Data Models. They specify technical rules and reusable IT implementation practices in IT-specific terms rather than shared executive/business language, so they are not dual.

Why are Visions focused on the long term?

They express what IT should deliver to support the organization's long-term business direction, typically 3–5 years ahead. They connect IT investment choices with long-term business outcomes rather than prescribing immediate project build details.

What are the two most important subtypes under Visions?

The two Essential subtypes are Business Capability Models (capabilities and their hierarchy/relationships) and Roadmaps (planned IT initiatives with direct business value).

Describe the artifacts under Landscapes.

Landscape Diagrams show the technical environment; Inventories catalog IT assets and properties; Enterprise Systems Portfolios map important systems to business capabilities; IT Roadmaps show planned technical initiatives. Together they help reuse assets and reduce duplication and legacy dependence.

What are IT Roadmaps under Landscapes?

Graphical plans of IT initiatives from a purely technical perspective, such as platform upgrades or migrations. Unlike Visions' Roadmaps, their emphasis is technical evolution rather than direct business-value communication.

What does the proper use of Outlines lead to?

Improved efficiency and ROI of IT investments: business-understandable descriptions help leaders assess the value and impact of proposed initiatives before implementation.

Describe the Options Assessments common artifacts under Outlines.

They evaluate alternatives for a particular IT initiative, supporting a business-understandable comparison of options and trade-offs. They are Common, business-focused change artifacts—not detailed build specifications.

What is the major difference between the two subtypes under Designs?

Solution Designs provide detailed technical/functional specifications; Preliminary Solution Designs are higher-level technical/functional designs. Both describe approved solutions. They differ mainly in detail, not whether the project has been approved.

How do successful EA practices utilize the 24 subtypes under CSVLOD?

Pragmatically select about 10–15 useful subtypes spread across the six general types rather than produce all 24 automatically. Adapt to real planning needs; some useful organizational artifacts may not fit the taxonomy neatly.
