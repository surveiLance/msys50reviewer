PART 5.1Architecture Analysis & Modeling

Start here: A model is a purposeful explanation of the enterprise, not a drawing of everything. Decide who needs to know what, capture the relevant facts, and check whether the explanation helps them make a decision.

Coverage: supplied 5.1 slides 4–21 and Module 5 class notes, PDF pages 48–54. All main lists and figure content are included below. Plain-language explanations and practice examples are original teaching aids. Required textbook readings are listed at the end, but were not available for verification.

Describing architecture: a connected explanation for decisions

Describing the architecture means representing the enterprise from an integrated perspective. Use a description technique or language that supports the right level of abstraction and integration, together with model-based analysis. Products, processes, applications, infrastructure, and their relationships should make sense together rather than form disconnected pictures.

The value of architectural models is realized when they are used in decision-making, especially to understand change and make well-informed design decisions. The class notes emphasize that producing an artifact is not the outcome by itself.

Original example: an enrollment diagram is useful when it helps decide which application change will reduce a registration bottleneck. A polished diagram nobody uses has not yet delivered that decision value.

Analysis: two separate ways to classify it

Functional analysis

Understand what the architecture does, assess the functional impact of change, and validate correctness. Ask: “Will this process still work?” It does not answer “How fast?” or “How cheap?”

Quantitative analysis

Measure effects: optimize alternative designs, assess change impacts, and plan capacity. Typical metrics are performance, reliability, and cost. Ask: “How much will response time improve?”

Do not confuse: both functional and quantitative analysis can assess change. The distinction is whether the question concerns functionality/correctness or measured properties. “Analytical” does not mean “quantitative.” The class notes mention KPIs and statistical data, but that does not make statistics a universal requirement of every quantitative technique. They also warn that not every nonfunctional requirement is already quantitative: “intuitive interface” still needs operational criteria if it is to be measured.

A model and the transformation it supports

A model is an unambiguous, abstract conception of selected parts or aspects of the real world. In EA, it represents products, business processes, applications, IT infrastructure, and relationships between them. Modeling is goal-driven; the architect chooses which aspects matter.

Models communicate. The aim is to introduce, agree on, and commit to a knowledge representation—not merely to finish a diagram.

Grice's Maxims: communicate enough, truthfully, relevantly, clearly

The lesson applies Grice's Maxims to optimized communication through models. Slide 13 contains the complete list as an image; all subpoints are unpacked here.

Quantity — enough, not excessMake the model as informative as necessary, but not more informative than necessary.
Quality — truth and evidenceDo not model what you believe false. Do not model claims for which you lack adequate evidence.
Relevance — serve the goalInclude things related to the modeling goal.
Manner — clear and orderlyAvoid obscure expression and ambiguity. Be brief: avoid unnecessary concepts/relations. Be orderly.

Original example: a correct enrollment model with hundreds of irrelevant printer details can violate quantity and relevance. An elegant model based on unsupported assumptions can violate quality. Unexplained symbols and tangled layout can violate manner.

Before modeling: seven checks and six choices

Ask all seven questions first (slide 14):

  1. Is there a clear stakeholder?
  2. Is the objective explicit?
  3. Will an EA model help achieve that objective?
  4. Are the boundaries of what to model clear?
  5. Is the intended situation as-is or to-be?
  6. Can the required information be obtained?
  7. Are expectations of the enterprise architect's role realistic?

Choose what to capture (slide 15): select goal-matched design viewpoints; focus; leave secondary matters and exceptions out where appropriate; be willing to abandon elements; discuss stable intermediate versions with stakeholders; start modeling from a single element.

These are selection principles, not permission to omit an exception that is essential to the goal. Starting from one element helps exploration; it does not mean the finished model must contain only one element.

Abstraction and six structural dimensions

Modeling breakdowns: readability versus effect

A modeling breakdown is a useful signal to investigate, not simply a reason to draw more boxes. Ask two different questions: “Was the message understood?” and “Did it have the intended effect?” (slides 19–21).

Readability — five problems
  • Not understood.
  • Understood in the wrong way.
  • No intuitive structure.
  • Unclear structure or notation.
  • Visualization distracts from the original message.
Effect — eight problems
  • Lacks status.
  • True but unwanted message.
  • Irrelevant.
  • Superfluous statements.
  • Too complex.
  • Too vague.
  • Insufficiently complete.
  • Not true.

Class-note example: someone can understand a model and still resist its implication of higher costs or job losses. That is a true-but-unwanted-message problem, not necessarily unreadable notation.

Source caution: the slide says “lacks status”; the class notes add “updates” and discuss nonresponse. These are not enough to establish a precise universal definition. Keep the slide phrase, but do not memorize “status means progress updates.” The notes' “center” is not a defined modeling term; sender/recipient is only a communication analogy.

Guide questions: Part 5.1

1. What does describing the architecture mean?

Represent the enterprise integratively using a description language with suitable abstraction and integration, plus model-based analysis. The model should support informed decisions about design and change, not merely document isolated details.

2. Functional versus quantitative analysis?

Functional analysis examines functionality, correctness, and functional change impacts. Quantitative analysis measures properties such as performance, reliability, and cost for optimization, impact assessment, and capacity planning. Both can assess change; only the latter answers numerical “how fast/how costly” questions.

3. What are the main goals of modeling?

Communicate a goal-relevant, unambiguous abstraction; introduce knowledge, agree on it, and obtain commitment to the knowledge representation. Ultimately use it for understanding, analysis, and decisions.

4. Describe each basic modeling activity.

Establish scope/purpose/focus; select viewpoints; create and structure the model; visualize it; use it; maintain it. Respectively decide boundaries and goal, choose lenses, organize content, present it, apply it, and keep it current.

5. Describe each modeling action.

Introduce adds content; refine adds relevant detail; abandon removes unneeded content; abstract suppresses detail to show essentials; translate expresses meaning in another representation; document modeling actions records changes and their rationale. Zoom-in/zoom-out is the class-note shorthand for refine/abstract.

6. What are Grice's Maxims?

Quantity: sufficient but not excessive information. Quality: avoid false or unsupported claims. Relevance: relate content to the modeling goal. Manner: avoid obscurity and ambiguity; be brief and orderly.

7. How do you know what to capture?

Start with the stakeholder, objective, boundaries, intended as-is/to-be situation, obtainable information, and realistic role expectations. Choose matching viewpoints, focus on essentials, remove secondary matter where appropriate, discuss stable intermediate versions, and start from a useful element. Maintain appropriate abstraction and goal-relative completeness.

8. Common structural dimensions?

Functionality: functional decomposition. Time: temporal structure/data flow/control flow. Usage: dependencies/call graphs. Location: physical distribution. Data structure: type/class hierarchies. Work: implementation units/module structure.

9. How do readability and effect cause modeling breakdowns?

Readability fails when a model is misunderstood or its structure/notation/presentation obscures the message. Effect fails when a representation is irrelevant, excessive, too complex/vague/incomplete/untrue, lacks status, or communicates an understood but unwanted truth. Understanding is not the same as acceptance or useful impact.

PART 5.2Views and Viewpoints

Start here: A viewpoint is the lens/toolbox; a view is the selected content seen through it; a visualization is how that content is presented. Choose using two separate questions: Why is the view needed? and How much of the architecture must it cover?

Coverage: supplied 5.2 slides 4–14 and Module 5 class notes, pages 54–58. Both image-only classification tables are retained. Examples in the tables are typical, not exclusive rules.

Model, view, viewpoint, and visualization

ModelThe underlying architectural concepts and relationships being represented.
ViewWhat a stakeholder sees: selected or derived architectural content addressing their concerns.
ViewpointWhere the stakeholder looks from: concepts, models, analysis techniques, and visualizations that provide a specialized lens/toolbox.
VisualizationThe presentation of a view—how the selected information is made visible.

Original example: the model contains systems and dependencies. A manager's view selects the dependencies relevant to a proposed change. A table and a diagram can present that view differently. Changing the presentation is not automatically changing the architectural meaning.

Start with stakeholder concerns

Slide 5 lists eight stakeholder groups whose concerns can shape viewpoints: upper-level management; middle-level management; end-user; architect; operational manager; project manager; system developer; system administrators.

The list names roles, not eight exhaustive definitions of their concerns. In a real modeling task, ask what the particular person needs to understand or decide.

Original concern examples: executives may need investment/change implications; an operational manager may need dependencies affecting service delivery; a developer may need implementation detail. The same person can have more than one concern.

Source reconciliation: the class notes say “system manager” and “system administration”; the exact slide labels are “System developer” and “System administrators.” Roles are the basis for choosing viewpoints, not themselves a complete viewpoint specification.

Choose purpose and content independently

Purpose asks what the view is for: designing, deciding, or informing. Content/abstraction asks what breadth it covers: details, coherence, or overview. Slide 10 combines purpose sectors with nested content bands. There is no compulsory Designing=Details, Deciding=Coherence, Informing=Overview pairing.

Original application: a landscape map used by a CEO to select a change can have Deciding purpose and Overview content. These answer different questions. A generic chart's appearance alone cannot prove its purpose.

Understand one cell, one strip, and a broader area

The class notes illustrate coverage using rows Business / Applications / Technology and columns IT / Marketing / HR. Those columns are illustrative departments, not the formal ArchiMate aspect taxonomy.

Details: one cellThe drawing highlights Technology × IT. Other individual cells could also illustrate one-layer/one-aspect coverage.
Coherence: one stripThe notes show alternative patterns: Technology across several columns, OR HR across several layers. One dimension expands.
Overview: broader areaBoth multiple layers and multiple aspects are included. The drawing covers the full grid, but an overview need not cover every cell.

Class emphasis: preserve the OR/AND distinction. Don't claim a majority percentage is required, or that overview is always better than details. The right coverage depends on the purpose and stakeholder.

Guide questions: Part 5.2

1. Define view and viewpoint.

A view is what a stakeholder sees: selected architectural content addressing concerns. A viewpoint describes the perspective/lens and its concepts, models, analysis techniques, and visualization tools. The view is a result of applying a suitable viewpoint, not the same thing as the viewpoint.

2. At least three stakeholder roles/concerns?

The slides name upper-level management, middle-level management, end-user, architect, operational manager, project manager, system developer, and system administrators. Their specific concerns shape the viewpoint; examples include decisions about change, operational dependencies, and implementation details, depending on the stakeholder.

3. Relationship between model, view, viewpoint, and visualization?

Select/derive a view from a model and visualize the view. The source includes update arrows Visualization → View and View → Model. A viewpoint guides selection/derivation and visualization, with input from architect and stakeholder. Do not conflate the underlying model with one displayed picture.

4. Choose viewpoints by purpose and content?

Purpose: Designing (design/navigate/compare alternatives), Deciding (support decisions), Informing (explain/convince/obtain commitment). Content: Details (one layer/one aspect), Coherence (multiple layers OR multiple aspects), Overview (multiple layers AND multiple aspects). Choose both independently; neither picture type nor job title determines everything.

5. At least two guidelines for using viewpoints?

Scoping selects viewpoints/subdomains/constraints. Creation selects relevant content. Validation checks agreement with the representation. Obtaining commitment checks agreement with potential impact. Informing disseminates to other stakeholders. In particular, validation and commitment are different checks.

PART 5.3Language for Enterprise Modeling

Start here: Services connect what a consumer needs to the implementation that provides it. ArchiMate gives concepts and relations for expressing this across layers. Remember who acts, what they do, what they act on—then distinguish the exposed service from its interface and internal implementation.

Coverage: supplied 5.3 slides 4–13 and Module 5 class notes, pages 58–62. Large source diagrams are explained as smaller labeled maps below. Ambiguous source notation is visible but excluded from graded relation-identification questions.

ArchiMate, services, and the three basic layers

The lesson presents ArchiMate as a formal, notation-based EA modeling language developed by The Open Group, supporting different abstraction layers. Archi, mentioned in the academic example, is the modeling tool used there—not a synonym for the language.

A service is a unit of functionality an entity makes available to its environment, with value for certain entities in that environment. Service orientation lets us distinguish different levels of granularity and organize EA models as a layered stack.

Business layerOffers products/services to customers. Business processes realize the business services.
Application layerSupports business through application services. Software application components realize those services.
Technology layerProvides infrastructural services needed by applications.

The layered stack: serving is not realizing

Memory cue: Realizes = implements the service. Serves = provides functionality to a consumer. A service is more abstract than its implementation; choosing granularity is part of deciding what to model.

Core pattern: internal/external and three aspects

Active structure — who acts?The actor, role, component, or other entity performing behavior.
Behavior — what happens?The action, task, or process performed.
Passive structure — what is acted on?The object or information that behavior accesses, such as a Student Record.

Class-note clarification: the helpful analogy is subject–verb–object, not the notes' “subject–verb–agreement.” Calling passive structure the “receiver” can mislead: a person receiving a service can still be active structure. Classify the modeled role, not just the everyday noun.

Service — what is provided?

Example from class: tuition payment functionality.

Interface — where/how it is exposed?

Class examples: cashier window or the user-facing interface of a payment portal. An entire portal system is not automatically just an Interface.

Original analogy: cashier (active structure) processes a payment (behavior), accessing a payment record (passive structure). Tuition payment is the exposed service; the cashier window is an interface. This is a teaching analogy, not an extra element in the source academic diagram.

Academic lifecycle example: read one layer at a time

Follow one grounded chain: Main Data Center serves RDBMS Server Cluster; the cluster realizes Database Storage Service; that service serves SIS; SIS realizes Student Registration Service; that service serves the first three business processes. These are different relation types, not one long chronological workflow.

Check your understanding: E-Learning Service has two realizing components, not only LMS. Student Registration Service serves three processes, not only Apply & Register. The picture supports these specific links; it does not say AWS stores every student record or that the admissions officer operates the LMS.

Guide questions: Part 5.3

1. How are the basic layers related through service orientation?

Business offers products/services to customers, realized by business processes. Application services support business behavior and are realized by application components. Infrastructural services support applications and are realized by technical infrastructure. Services link layers; internal services can also connect components within one layer. Serving supplies functionality; realization implements the service.

2. What are ArchiMate's core elements?

Examples include business actor/process, application component, equipment, and service. Core organization combines architecture layers, internal/external abstraction, and active structure/behavior/passive structure. In the core diagram, internal active/behavior/passive elements connect to external Interface/Service concepts. Elements are connected through defined relations, not arbitrary arrows.

3. Name three typical relations.

The slide names six: Association (generic), Realizes, Serves (provides functionality to), Flow, Triggers, and Access. For example, an application component realizes a service; the service serves business behavior; behavior accesses a passive object. Distinguish their arrow/line notations.

4. How are core and extended concepts used with viewpoints?

Identify stakeholders/concerns; determine purpose/scope/viewpoint; choose relevant elements/relations/rules; construct the model. Select from the core or applicable focused concept groups as needed for that viewpoint, rather than drawing every concept. The slides list focused groups and references, but do not provide all their detailed definitions.
